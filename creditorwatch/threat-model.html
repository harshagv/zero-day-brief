Threat model: a generic SaaS credit bureau

Not your architecture — I don't have it. This is the shape of the problem your business model implies, walked through the way I'd actually walk through a threat model on day one. Correct me where it's wrong; that's the point of doing this live.

What this is, precisely: a reference architecture built from public-domain patterns of a commercial credit-reporting SaaS platform, plus the scope your JD names — SaaS platforms, endpoints, CI/CD, application layer. What this isn't: any claim about CreditorWatch's actual systems, vendors, or controls.

01Why this business model has a distinct threat profile

Three things make a credit bureau's attack surface different from a generic SaaS app: the core asset (credit data) is toxic if leaked and valuable if tampered with; the data arrives from third parties you don't fully control; and the output — a credit decision — is itself a target for abuse (score-shopping, enumeration, manipulation), not just a thing to protect at rest.

02The architecture, and where I'd expect the pressure points

Two paths matter more than the diagram makes obvious at a glance: the amber dashed line is the deploy path (CI/CD into production) and the red dotted line is the employee-to-admin-tools lateral path. In most enterprise SaaS breaches, that red line is how the attacker actually got there — not the front door.

04If I only had 90 days, in this order

Ranked by impact × likelihood, not by what's easiest to ship first.

  • 1

    T4 — Close the data store exposure path

    It's the one asset where "breached" and "business-ending" are the same sentence for a credit bureau.

    Why first: everything else on this diagram exists to protect this one box. Fix the boundary before optimizing anything upstream of it.

  • 2

    T6 — Close the employee-to-admin lateral path

    Most enterprise SaaS breaches start with a compromised endpoint or SaaS account, not a zero-day against the app.

    Why second: this is the path that turns a phished laptop into a data-store incident. It's also the fastest to underestimate because it doesn't show up in an AppSec review.

  • 3

    T5 — Lock down the CI/CD supply chain

    The deploy path controls everything that ends up in the application zone — including anything that touches T4 and T3.

    Why third: it's the multiplier. A gate here catches problems before they become T1–T4 incidents, and it's the pattern I've already shipped twice (Mox Bank, Modern Group).
